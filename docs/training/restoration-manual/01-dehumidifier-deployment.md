# Module 01 — Dehumidifier Deployment: Contractor Responsibilities & Risk Management

**Part of:** CCW Restoration Training Manual
**Audience:** Restoration contractors, technicians, and supervisors deploying dehumidification equipment on water-damage and structural-drying jobs.
**Status:** Reference training material.

## Why this module exists

A dehumidifier is a water-removal machine. Whatever the make or model — including
premium LGR units such as the Dri-Eaz BD2500 — every dehumidifier condenses large
volumes of water and then has to discharge that water somewhere. A manufacturing fault
can happen, but the far more common cause of a secondary-damage event is how the unit
was **deployed, routed, secured, and monitored** on site.

This module sets out where accepted restoration practice places the contractor's
responsibilities across the full life of a deployment — from installation, through
long-term operation, to the point where equipment is declared no longer required but
is still physically on the property.

The structure below follows the ten questions a restorer would be asked if giving an
independent expert opinion on deployment practice. Each section states the accepted
practice and why it matters.

---

## 1. Duty of care

A professional restoration contractor installing dehumidification equipment inside an
occupied residential property owes an ongoing duty to:

- **Install the equipment safely** so that its normal, foreseeable operation does not
  create a new hazard or a new water source.
- **Protect the occupants and the property** — the building and its contents — from
  harm arising from the equipment the contractor chose to place there.
- **Manage the equipment for the duration it remains deployed**, not only at the moment
  of installation. Deploying water-removal plant in a home is an ongoing responsibility,
  not a one-off event.
- **Communicate and document** — make clear who is responsible for the equipment, what
  the occupant should and should not touch, and what happens if a problem is noticed.

The duty is proportionate to the foreseeable risk. Water-removal equipment left running
unattended in an occupied home carries obvious foreseeable risk, so the standard of care
expected is correspondingly high.

**Teaching point:** the duty does not end when the machine is switched on. It runs for as
long as the contractor's equipment is on site under the contractor's arrangement.

---

## 2. Installation — good industry practice

Good practice at installation is about making a foreseeable failure either unlikely or
harmless. A competent installation addresses:

- **Drain hose routing** — the shortest practical run with continuous fall to the
  discharge point. Avoid uphill sections, long unsupported spans, and routes that force
  the hose across a walkway or through a doorway that will be opened and closed.
- **Avoiding restriction or crushing** — never route a hose where a door, furniture,
  trades, or foot traffic can pinch, kink, or crush it. A hose routed **through a
  sliding or swinging door** is a classic restriction point: the door closes on the hose
  and blocks discharge, and the condensate then backs up or overflows. This is a
  foreseeable, avoidable failure.
- **Securing hoses** — fix the hose so it cannot fall off the drain point, work loose, or
  be dragged out of position. Secure both ends and support the run.
- **Preventing accidental movement** — position the unit where it is stable and out of the
  main traffic path so it is not easily bumped, moved, or unplugged.
- **Discharge location** — discharge to an appropriate point (floor waste, laundry tub,
  external drain) that can accept continuous condensate. Confirm the discharge point
  actually drains and will not overflow.
- **Minimising consequences of a leak** — assume the discharge *could* fail, and place
  the unit and route the hose so that if it does, the water lands somewhere tolerable
  (a wet area, over a drain, on a hard floor) rather than over timber flooring, carpet,
  or into a cavity.

**Teaching point:** design the install so that the worst realistic failure is contained,
not catastrophic.

---

## 3. Foreseeable risks — what an experienced restorer anticipates

An experienced restorer treats the following as **expected** failure modes, not
surprises, and installs and monitors accordingly:

- Blocked or partially blocked drain hoses
- Kinked, crushed, or pinched hoses
- Disconnected hoses (worked loose or knocked off)
- Accidental movement of the unit
- Occupants or trades altering the installation
- Condensate pump failures
- Float-switch / float-mechanism failures
- Power interruptions (unit stops, or restarts in an unexpected state)
- Poor hose routing / inadequate fall
- Inadequate or unreliable drainage arrangements
- Extended unattended operation over long periods

Because these are foreseeable, the professional response is not "this shouldn't happen"
— it is "this can happen, so what have we done to prevent it and to catch it early."

**Teaching point:** foreseeability is the test. If a competent restorer could reasonably
anticipate a failure mode, managing it is part of the job.

---

## 4. Long-term deployments

Leaving dehumidifiers running for extended periods does occur, but it does **not** reduce
the contractor's obligations — if anything, a longer deployment raises the monitoring
burden, because there is more time for hoses to shift, drains to block, and conditions to
change.

Where equipment is deployed over weeks or months, accepted practice is:

- **Regular documented inspections.** Attend the site, physically check the unit, the
  hose run, the discharge point, and the surrounding area. Confirm the unit is operating
  and draining.
- **Document each inspection** — date, who attended, what was checked, readings taken,
  condition of hose and discharge, and any action. Photographs are strong evidence.
- **A defined frequency.** Frequency should match the risk and be agreed and recorded; on
  a long unattended deployment, inspection intervals should be short enough that a
  developing fault is caught before it causes damage. If a unit is genuinely left for
  long periods, the inspection regime is what makes that defensible.
- **Clear ownership.** One party is responsible for monitoring, and everyone knows who it
  is. Ambiguity about "whose job it was to check" is itself a failure.

**Teaching point:** long unattended operation is only defensible when it is paired with a
documented inspection regime. Unmonitored long-term operation is the risk.

---

## 5. Environmental control vs active structural drying

A contractor may argue that equipment retained for **environmental / humidity control**
carries lighter obligations than equipment used for **active structural drying**. In
practice the distinction changes the drying goal, but it does **not** remove the physical
and installation obligations:

- The unit still condenses and discharges water. The **installation, hose-routing,
  securing, discharge, and secondary-containment obligations are identical**, because the
  physical failure modes are identical.
- "Operating automatically on humidity" does not mean unattended-and-unchecked. Automatic
  operation controls *when* it runs; it does nothing to prevent a blocked hose, a failed
  float, or a crushed drain line.
- The monitoring obligation may change in *what* is measured (ambient conditions rather
  than material moisture content) but not in *whether* the deployment is inspected and the
  water-discharge path verified.

**Teaching point:** environmental control changes the drying objective, not the duty to
install and manage the water-removal equipment safely.

---

## 6. Equipment left after completion

When equipment has been declared no longer required — for example, the contractor is
advised it can be collected — but it physically remains on the property for an extended
period, responsibility does not simply evaporate:

- **The equipment remains the contractor's plant, deployed under the contractor's
  arrangement, until it is removed or responsibility is formally handed over.** Ownership
  and control of the machine ordinarily stays with the party that installed it.
- **Prompt removal is itself part of the duty.** Once equipment is no longer needed,
  accepted practice is to retrieve it promptly. Leaving live water-removal plant running
  in an occupied home after it is no longer required extends the risk window
  unnecessarily.
- **If removal is delayed, the monitoring obligation continues.** Equipment that is still
  running is still a live risk and should still be inspected — being "finished with" is not
  the same as being safe to ignore.
- **The property owner / insurer** may carry responsibilities too (e.g. providing access,
  not interfering), but the contractor's responsibility for its own energised plant is not
  discharged merely by sending a notice that the equipment can be collected.

**Teaching point:** "no longer required" is a trigger to *remove* the equipment, not a
licence to leave it running unmonitored.

---

## 7. Alteration by others

If a third party (occupant, another trade, unknown person) relocates the unit or reroutes
the drain hose without the contractor's knowledge, this is significant from a restoration
perspective:

- **It introduces new, uncontrolled risks.** A relocated unit or a rerouted hose may now
  discharge to nowhere, run uphill, be kinked, or pass through a door — the exact failure
  modes a proper install was designed to avoid.
- **It can compromise the original installation.** An install is only as safe as its
  current physical state. Once altered by others, the contractor's original safe setup no
  longer describes what is actually on site.
- **But it does not automatically absolve the contractor**, and here the two duties meet:
  interference by others is *itself a foreseeable risk* (see §3). Accepted practice
  anticipates that occupants and trades may move things, and manages for it — by securing
  the install, positioning the unit out of the way, briefing occupants, and inspecting
  regularly enough to catch alterations before they cause damage. Whether an alteration
  shifts responsibility depends on the specific facts: what was altered, whether it was
  foreseeable, whether the contractor's inspection regime should have detected it, and how
  long it went unchecked.

**Teaching point:** third-party interference is real and matters — and it is also exactly
the kind of foreseeable event a competent deployment and inspection regime is meant to
anticipate and catch.

## 8. Secondary-damage prevention

Competent restorers assume a discharge failure *can* occur and build defences so that when
it does, damage is minimised. Measures include:

- **Hose protection** — route away from crush/kink/traffic points; sleeve or guard where a
  hose must cross a hazard; never through a closing door.
- **Secondary containment** where appropriate — a drip tray, bund, or catch under the unit
  and at the discharge point so an overflow is captured rather than reaching flooring or
  cavities.
- **Sound drain selection** — discharge to a point that reliably accepts continuous
  condensate and cannot back up.
- **Positioning** — place the unit over or beside a wet area / hard floor / drain, not over
  timber, carpet, or a subfloor cavity, so a failure lands somewhere tolerable.
- **Inspection regimes** — documented, regular checks (see §4) to catch a developing fault
  early.
- **Alarms / monitoring aids** where the deployment is long or unattended — water/leak
  alarms, float-full alarms, or remote monitoring reduce the time a fault goes unnoticed.

**Teaching point:** you cannot guarantee a machine will never fail; you *can* deploy it so
that a failure is caught early and contained.

## 9. Recognised standards and guidance

Restorers should work to recognised references rather than personal habit. The primary
references for water-damage work and equipment deployment include:

- **IICRC S500 — Standard for Professional Water Damage Restoration.** The recognised
  industry standard covering water-damage restoration practice, including drying,
  equipment use, and monitoring/documentation expectations.
  <https://iicrc.org/s500/>
- **IICRC S520 — Standard for Professional Mold Remediation** (where secondary microbial
  growth is a risk from prolonged moisture). <https://iicrc.org/s520/>
- **Restoration Industry Association (RIA)** — industry body publications and guidance on
  professional restoration practice. <https://restorationindustry.org/>
- **Applicable Australian Standards** and the manufacturer's operating manual and
  installation instructions for the specific unit, which form part of accepted practice.
- **Work Health & Safety** obligations relating to plant deployed in an occupied building.

> Always work to the current published edition of any standard. Do not present training
> material as a substitute for the specific standard, the manufacturer's instructions, or
> certified professional judgement on a given job.

**Teaching point:** "accepted practice" is not a matter of opinion — it is anchored to
published standards, the manufacturer's instructions, and documented professional method.

## 10. The boundary — manufacturer vs contractor responsibility

The clearest way to frame the boundary:

- **The manufacturer** is responsible for the **product itself** — that the unit, as
  designed and built, is fit for purpose and free from manufacturing defect, and that it
  is supplied with adequate operating and safety information.
- **The restoration contractor** is responsible for **deploying and managing that product
  safely** on the job — selecting an appropriate unit, installing it correctly, routing
  and securing the drain, choosing a sound discharge, containing foreseeable failures,
  monitoring the deployment, removing it promptly when no longer required, and responding
  to foreseeable events including third-party interference.

A manufacturing defect and a deployment failure are separate questions and can each exist
independently. A unit can be defective *and* poorly deployed; a perfectly sound unit can
still cause damage through poor deployment. The contractor's responsibility is the
**safe deployment and ongoing management** of whatever equipment they place in a home —
that responsibility exists regardless of whether the product also turns out to be
defective.

**Teaching point:** the manufacturer owns the box; the contractor owns how the box is
used, placed, drained, watched, and removed.

## Deployment checklist (field summary)

Use this as the quick on-site discipline behind the module.

**At installation**
- [ ] Unit positioned stable, out of main traffic, over a tolerable-failure surface
- [ ] Drain hose: shortest run, continuous fall, no uphill sections
- [ ] Hose not through a door, not across a crush/kink/traffic point
- [ ] Both hose ends secured; run supported
- [ ] Discharge point confirmed to actually drain and accept continuous condensate
- [ ] Secondary containment (tray/bund) under unit / at discharge where appropriate
- [ ] Leak/float alarm fitted for long or unattended deployments
- [ ] Occupant briefed: what not to touch, who to call, what to report

**During deployment**
- [ ] Documented inspections at an interval matched to the risk
- [ ] Each visit: unit running, hose intact and in place, discharge draining, area dry
- [ ] Photographs and readings recorded
- [ ] Any alteration by others noted, corrected, and documented

**At end / when no longer required**
- [ ] Equipment retrieved promptly once declared no longer needed
- [ ] While still on site and running, inspection regime continues
- [ ] Removal / handover of responsibility recorded
