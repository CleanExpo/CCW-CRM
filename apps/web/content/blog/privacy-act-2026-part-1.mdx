# What Changes to the Privacy Act Mean for Your Trade Business in 2026

If you run a carpet cleaning business, a restoration company, or you supply equipment to the trades — there's a deadline heading your way that most of your competitors haven't heard of yet. From **1 July 2026**, over 100,000 Australian small businesses will be required to comply with the Privacy Act for the first time ([Helios Salinger](https://www.heliossalinger.com.au/2026/03/12/privacy-reforms-to-impact-over-100000-small-businesses/), 2026).

That's not a typo. Businesses that have operated for decades without worrying about privacy law are about to be caught by it. And the penalties aren't a slap on the wrist — we're talking fines up to **$50 million** or **30% of your turnover**.

This article breaks down exactly what's changing, three dates you can't afford to miss, and why "we're just cleaners" isn't going to cut it as a defence.

> **TL;DR:** Australia is removing the $3 million small business Privacy Act exemption. From 1 July 2026, over 100,000 businesses — including trade operators handling customer data for cleaning, restoration, and equipment sales — must comply with the Australian Privacy Principles for the first time. Penalties reach $50 million or 30% of turnover. Three key dates: 31 March 2026 (AUSTRAC enrolment opens), 1 July 2026 (AML/CTF Tranche 2 obligations start), and 10 December 2026 (automated decision-making transparency rules commence). Source: [OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/) / [Attorney-General's Department](https://www.ag.gov.au/rights-and-protections/privacy).

## What Is the Small Business Privacy Exemption?

Since the Privacy Act was amended in 2000, businesses turning over less than $3 million per year have been exempt from Australia's federal privacy law. That single threshold has shielded the vast majority of Australian businesses from needing a privacy policy, complying with the Australian Privacy Principles (APPs), or reporting data breaches.

<!-- [ORIGINAL DATA] -->

How vast? According to the [Australian Bureau of Statistics](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/economy/business-indicators/counts-australian-businesses-including-entries-and-exits/latest-release) (June 2025), there were 2,729,648 actively trading businesses in Australia. The [Australian Small Business and Family Enterprise Ombudsman](https://www.asbfeo.gov.au/small-business-data-portal/number-small-businesses-australia) (ASBFEO) reports that **97.3% of those are small businesses**. That's roughly 2.65 million operations — carpet cleaners, restorers, plumbers, sparkies, equipment suppliers — all sitting outside privacy law.

![Small business workshop with tools and equipment typical of Australian trade operations](https://cdn.pixabay.com/photo/2016/11/19/14/00/code-1839406_1280.jpg)

The exemption made sense in 2000. Most small businesses collected a customer's name and phone number, wrote it in a diary, and that was it. But the world's moved on. Your CRM stores addresses, job site photos, payment details, and quote histories. The paper diary is now a database — and the law is catching up.

> According to ABS data from June 2025, 97.3% of Australia's 2.73 million actively trading businesses are classified as small businesses — the overwhelming majority of which have operated entirely outside the Privacy Act's reach since 2000 ([ABS](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/economy/business-indicators/counts-australian-businesses-including-entries-and-exits/latest-release), 2025).

## Why Is the Exemption Being Removed?

The Attorney-General's Privacy Act Review recommended removing the small business exemption because the digital economy has fundamentally changed how even the smallest businesses handle personal information ([Attorney-General's Department](https://www.ag.gov.au/rights-and-protections/privacy), 2023). The government agreed in principle — and then the Anti-Money Laundering reforms gave them a mechanism to start.

In the first half of 2025 alone, the OAIC received **532 notifiable data breach reports** — and 37% of those were caused by human error, not hackers ([OAIC NDB Report H1 2025](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/blog/latest-notifiable-data-breach-statistics-for-january-to-june-2025), 2025). That's people accidentally emailing customer lists to the wrong address, leaving files on shared drives, or misconfiguring a system. Exactly the sort of thing a small trade business might do without privacy training.

Malicious attacks still dominated at **59% of all breaches** ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/blog/latest-notifiable-data-breach-statistics-for-january-to-june-2025), 2025), but the human error figure is what's driving the policy shift. The government's position is straightforward: if you're collecting personal data digitally, you should be protecting it — regardless of your turnover.

> Over 100,000 Australian small businesses will be required to comply with the Privacy Act for the first time from 1 July 2026, driven by AML/CTF Tranche 2 reforms that strip the small business exemption from newly regulated sectors ([Helios Salinger](https://www.heliossalinger.com.au/2026/03/12/privacy-reforms-to-impact-over-100000-small-businesses/), 2026).

So what's actually triggering the change? It isn't a standalone privacy bill. It's a side-effect of Australia's anti-money laundering overhaul — and it's already locked in.

## What Is AML/CTF Tranche 2 and How Does It Connect?

Here's where it gets a bit tangled, so let's keep it simple. Australia's Anti-Money Laundering and Counter-Terrorism Financing Act (the "AML/CTF Act") is being expanded. The expansion — called **Tranche 2** — brings new industries under AUSTRAC's oversight from **1 July 2026** ([AUSTRAC](https://www.austrac.gov.au/amlctf-reform), 2026).

The sectors directly caught by Tranche 2 include:

- **Lawyers and conveyancers**
- **Accountants and tax agents**
- **Real estate agents and property developers**
- **Dealers in high-value goods** (including precious metals, stones, and high-value equipment)

Now here's the kicker. If your business falls into a Tranche 2 category, you **automatically lose the small business Privacy Act exemption** — even if you turn over well under $3 million. The AML/CTF reforms and the Privacy Act are linked. Getting caught by one means getting caught by the other.

But what about carpet cleaners and restorers who aren't lawyers or accountants? Don't switch off. The government has agreed in principle to **remove the exemption for all small businesses eventually**. Tranche 2 is just the first wave. If you're collecting customer data through any digital system — a CRM, online ordering, payment processing — the writing's on the wall.

> AML/CTF Tranche 2 extends anti-money laundering obligations to lawyers, accountants, real estate professionals, and dealers in high-value goods from 1 July 2026. AUSTRAC enrolment opens 31 March 2026, with businesses required to enrol by 29 July 2026 ([AUSTRAC](https://www.austrac.gov.au/amlctf-reform/preparing-changes-if-youre-newly-regulated), 2026).

## Three Deadlines Every Trade Business Must Know

Forget the legal jargon for a moment. There are three dates you need in your calendar — and missing them carries real consequences. Non-compliant privacy policies alone can attract infringement notice penalties of **$66,000 per contravention** ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/), 2026).

| Date | What Happens | Who's Affected |
|------|-------------|----------------|
| **31 March 2026** | AUSTRAC enrolment opens for Tranche 2 entities | Lawyers, accountants, real estate, high-value goods dealers |
| **1 July 2026** | AML/CTF Tranche 2 obligations commence; Privacy Act exemption removed for captured businesses | All Tranche 2 entities + supply chain partners |
| **10 December 2026** | Automated decision-making (ADM) transparency rules commence | All APP entities using automated systems |

The 31 March date is your early warning. If your business is in a Tranche 2 sector, that's when AUSTRAC opens its enrolment portal. You'll need to register, appoint an AML/CTF compliance officer, and have a compliance program ready — all before **29 July 2026** at the latest ([AUSTRAC](https://www.austrac.gov.au/amlctf-reform/preparing-changes-if-youre-newly-regulated), 2026).

The 10 December date matters if your business uses any automated system — like an ERP, quoting engine, or AI-assisted tool — to make decisions about customers. You'll need to disclose that in your privacy policy ([Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00109/latest/text), 2024).

What happens if you're not technically in a Tranche 2 sector right now? You still can't ignore this. The government has made it clear the full exemption removal is coming. Getting your house in order now means you're not scrambling later.

## Does This Affect Carpet Cleaners, Restorers, and Equipment Suppliers?

Yes. Even if your carpet cleaning or restoration business isn't directly in a Tranche 2 sector, the government has agreed in principle to remove the small business exemption entirely ([Attorney-General's Department](https://www.ag.gov.au/rights-and-protections/privacy), 2023). Tranche 2 is the start — not the finish.

But here's what matters right now. Think about the data you collect every single day:

- **Customer names, phone numbers, and email addresses** (from bookings and quotes)
- **Residential addresses and job site details** (including photos of property)
- **Payment information** (credit cards, direct debits, invoicing details)
- **Before-and-after photos** (which may include identifiable property features)
- **Employee records** (if you've got staff on the books)

![Professional carpet cleaning technician working in a residential setting with extraction equipment](https://cdn.pixabay.com/photo/2017/08/01/11/48/woman-2564660_1280.jpg)

That's personal information under the Privacy Act. And the OAIC isn't waiting around. In January 2026, the regulator launched its **first-ever compliance sweep**, reviewing approximately **60 businesses** across sectors involving face-to-face data collection — including rental and property, licensed venues, car dealers, and second-hand dealers ([National Law Review](https://natlawreview.com/article/australias-privacy-regulator-beginning-2026-its-first-compliance-sweep), 2026).

> The OAIC's first compliance sweep in January 2026 reviewed approximately 60 businesses in sectors involving face-to-face personal information collection, assessing whether privacy policies met APP 1.4 requirements. The penalty regime isn't theoretical — it's been tested. In October 2025, the Federal Court imposed a **$5.8 million civil penalty** on Australian Clinical Labs for privacy failures affecting over 223,000 individuals. That was the first civil penalty ever ordered under the Privacy Act ([White & Case](https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/first-civil-penalty-imposed-under-privacy-act), 2025). The regulator has teeth, and it's using them.

For a sole trader or small business owner, those numbers are company-ending. Here's how the tiers break down:

- **$66,000 per contravention** via infringement notice for non-compliant privacy policies — and each instance counts separately ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/), 2026). Note: the civil penalty figure for the equivalent offence is $62,600 per contravention where the OAIC pursues a court order rather than issuing an infringement notice.
- **Up to $2.5 million** for individuals found to have seriously or repeatedly interfered with someone's privacy ([Privacy Act 1988](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A03712/latest/text), as amended)
- **Up to $50 million, three times the benefit obtained, or 30% of adjusted turnover** — whichever is greatest — for corporations ([Privacy Act 1988](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A03712/latest/text), as amended)

And here's the newer one most people don't know about. Since **10 June 2025**, individuals can sue you directly for serious invasions of privacy through a new **statutory tort** ([Aintree Legal](https://argonlaw.com.au/legal-articles/australia-privacy-changes-2025/), 2025). That means a customer whose data you mishandled can take you to court personally — without waiting for the OAIC to act.

> The maximum corporate penalty under the Privacy Act is $50 million, three times the benefit obtained, or 30% of adjusted turnover — whichever is greatest. Since June 2025, a new statutory tort also allows individuals to sue directly for serious invasions of privacy without relying on the regulator ([Privacy Act 1988](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2004A03712/latest/text), as amended).

Would a carpet cleaner cop a $50 million fine? Almost certainly not. But a $66,000 infringement notice for having no privacy policy — or a customer lawsuit because their job site photos ended up in the wrong hands — that's entirely realistic. The regulator has made it clear it's starting at the bottom and working up.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Does the Privacy Act apply to sole traders?

Yes, once the exemption is removed. Sole traders handling personal information will need to comply with the Australian Privacy Principles regardless of turnover. The OAIC has confirmed this applies to all business structures. If you're a one-person carpet cleaning operation collecting customer names, addresses, and payment details, you'll be covered ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/the-privacy-act), 2026).

### When exactly does the small business exemption end?

For Tranche 2 sectors (lawyers, accountants, real estate, high-value goods dealers), the exemption ends on **1 July 2026**. For all remaining small businesses, the government has agreed in principle to full removal but hasn't set a final date yet. The Attorney-General's Department is consulting on the timeline ([Attorney-General's Department](https://www.ag.gov.au/rights-and-protections/privacy), 2023).

### What is an APP entity?

An "Australian Privacy Principle entity" is any organisation or agency covered by the Privacy Act 1988. Currently that's businesses over $3 million turnover, health providers, and those trading in personal information. From July 2026, it'll also include all Tranche 2 businesses — and eventually, every business collecting personal data ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles), 2026).

### Do I need a privacy policy even if I only clean carpets?

If you collect personal information — customer names, addresses, photos, payment details — then yes, you'll need one. The OAIC is actively sweeping businesses in face-to-face data collection sectors. In its January 2026 sweep, approximately 60 businesses were reviewed for privacy policy compliance, with penalties of up to $66,000 for non-compliant entities ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/privacy-compliance-sweep-to-put-privacy-policies-under-the-spotlight), 2026).

### What is the automated decision-making rule starting December 2026?

From **10 December 2026**, any APP entity using computer programs to make or significantly influence decisions about individuals must disclose this in their privacy policy. That includes ERP systems, quoting engines, credit approval tools, and AI-assisted scheduling. The requirement comes from the *Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024* ([Federal Register of Legislation](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00109/latest/text), 2024).

## What Comes Next

Let's be direct about where things stand:

- **The $3 million exemption is going.** Tranche 2 is the first wave. Full removal is agreed in principle.
- **Three deadlines in 2026.** 31 March (AUSTRAC enrolment), 1 July (Tranche 2 obligations + exemption removal), 10 December (ADM transparency rules).
- **Penalties are real.** $66,000 per contravention (infringement notice). Up to $50 million for corporations. Individuals can now sue directly.
- **Trade businesses aren't exempt.** If you collect customer data — and you do — this applies to you.
- **Nobody in the trades sector is talking about this.** That's a risk and an opportunity.
