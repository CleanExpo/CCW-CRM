# The POLA Act 2024: What Australia's New Automated Decision-Making Rules Mean for Equipment Suppliers

If you run an equipment supply, trade services, or maintenance business in Australia, there's a compliance deadline you need on your radar: **10 December 2026**.

That's when new automated decision-making (ADM) disclosure requirements under the *Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024* (Cth) — commonly called the POLA Act — come into force. The rules amend the *Privacy Act 1988* (Cth) to require businesses to be transparent with customers about when algorithms, AI tools, or automated systems are making decisions that affect them.

If your business uses an ERP, quoting tool, credit-approval system, or AI assistant to make or significantly influence decisions about customers or employees, you likely need to update your Privacy Policy before the deadline.

---

## What Is the POLA Act 2024?

The *Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024* (Cth) received Royal Assent on 10 December 2024. It is the federal government's first major overhaul of Australian privacy law in over a decade and introduces several significant reforms to the *Privacy Act 1988*:

- A new statutory tort for serious invasions of privacy
- A Children's Online Privacy Code framework
- Clearer requirements around overseas data disclosure
- **New Australian Privacy Principle (APP) 1.8**: a mandatory disclosure obligation when automated decision-making is used with personal information

APP 1.8 is the provision most relevant to equipment and trade suppliers. It takes effect **24 months after Royal Assent** — that is, 10 December 2026.

---

## Who Has to Comply?

APP 1.8 applies to **APP entities** — broadly, any organisation or individual that is subject to the Australian Privacy Principles under the *Privacy Act 1988*.

This generally includes:

- Businesses with an annual turnover greater than **$3 million**
- Health service providers (regardless of turnover)
- Businesses that trade in personal information
- Certain government-related bodies

If your equipment supply business is below the $3M turnover threshold and does not handle health information or trade in personal data, you may fall outside the APPs entirely. However, as your business grows — and as ERP adoption accelerates across the trade sector — many previously exempt businesses will cross this threshold.

**The Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC)** recommends that smaller businesses voluntarily adopt privacy-compliant practices even if not currently required, as future threshold changes are under active consideration by the federal government.

> **Check your status:** The OAIC's [privacy threshold assessment tool](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/your-privacy-rights/your-privacy-rights-overview) can help you determine whether the APPs currently apply to your business.

---

## What Is "Automated Decision-Making" Under the POLA Act?

The Act defines an automated decision as one made, or significantly influenced by, a computer system with **little or no human involvement** — where the decision has a significant effect on the rights or interests of the individual.

For equipment and trade suppliers, common scenarios include:

| System / Tool | Automated Decision | Significant Effect? |
|---|---|---|
| ERP credit limit module | Auto-approve or reject credit terms for a trade account | Yes — affects customer's ability to purchase on account |
| Quoting engine with AI pricing | Automatically prices jobs or materials based on customer history | Potentially — if different customers receive materially different terms |
| Job scheduling AI | Routes service jobs to technicians based on customer priority tier | Potentially — affects response time and service levels |
| Inventory allocation logic | Prioritises order fulfilment by customer segment | Potentially — affects delivery timeline |
| HR / leave management software | Auto-approves or rejects employee leave requests | Yes — directly affects employment conditions |

The key test is not whether a human could theoretically override the system — it is whether, **in practice**, the system's output drives the outcome with little or no genuine human review.

---

## What Does APP 1.8 Actually Require?

From 10 December 2026, if your business uses ADM with personal information, your **Privacy Policy must include a statement** disclosing:

1. **That you use automated decision-making** with personal information
2. **The kinds of personal information** used in the process
3. **The nature of the decisions** and how they significantly affect individuals
4. **Whether individuals can request human review** of an automated decision (and if so, how)

The disclosure does not require you to reveal your algorithms or intellectual property. It requires transparency — customers and employees should not be surprised to learn a machine, not a person, determined their credit limit or job priority.

The OAIC has signalled it will publish updated APP 1 guidelines before the December 2026 deadline to assist businesses with compliant Privacy Policy language.

## Why This Matters More Than You Might Think

Many trade and equipment suppliers have been rolling out ERP and AI-assisted tools rapidly over the past two years. The efficiency gains are real: faster quoting, automated reorder points, dynamic pricing, AI-assisted customer service.

But this adoption has generally outpaced internal governance. Most businesses in the sector have not updated their Privacy Policies since they first drafted them — often a generic template written before any of these tools existed.

The risk is not just regulatory. Customers — particularly large construction contractors, facility managers, and government buyers — are increasingly asking suppliers about data practices as part of their vendor due diligence. A Privacy Policy that is silent on ADM, or that was last updated in 2019, is a procurement risk.

The OAIC can investigate complaints and issue determinations. Under the *Privacy Act 1988*, non-compliance with the APPs can result in:

- Formal determinations requiring policy changes
- Civil penalty applications to the Federal Court for serious or repeated contraventions (up to $2.22 million for individuals, $50 million or more for organisations, depending on circumstances)
- Reputational damage from published determinations

The civil penalty provisions apply to **serious or repeated interferences with privacy** — a first-time failure to update a Privacy Policy is unlikely to trigger a penalty. But a pattern of non-compliance, or a failure to disclose ADM after a customer complaint, is a different matter.

## What Should You Do Now?

You have until **10 December 2026** — roughly 18 months from now. That is enough time to act without rushing, but not enough time to forget about it.

**Immediate steps (no cost, can do this week):**

1. **Inventory your automated tools.** List every software system that makes or substantially influences decisions about customers or employees. Include your ERP, quoting tools, CRM, job scheduling software, and any AI assistant integrations.

2. **Assess significance.** For each tool, ask: does the output affect someone's credit terms, service priority, pricing, employment, or access to products? If yes, it likely qualifies as ADM under the POLA Act.

3. **Review your current Privacy Policy.** Is it up to date? Does it mention any of these tools? If your policy predates your ERP rollout, it almost certainly needs updating.

**Before December 2026:**

4. **Update your Privacy Policy** to include an ADM disclosure statement. Your legal adviser or the OAIC's updated APP 1 guidance (expected mid-2026) will provide compliant template language.

5. **Document your ADM processes.** Note what personal information each automated system uses, what decisions it makes, and whether human override is available. This documentation supports your disclosure and demonstrates good-faith compliance.

6. **Train your team.** Customer-facing staff should be able to explain (in plain language) how your systems work and who a customer can contact if they want a human to review a decision.

---

## The Bigger Picture

APP 1.8 is part of a broader shift in Australian privacy law toward **transparency and accountability** in AI and automated systems. Future reform tranches — currently under consultation — are expected to introduce rights to request explanations for automated decisions and potentially a right to opt out in certain contexts.

Businesses that treat December 2026 as a checkbox exercise will find themselves updating their policies again in 2027 and 2028. Those that use it as an opportunity to genuinely document and govern their ADM practices will be better positioned for each subsequent round of reform.

For equipment and trade suppliers, the message is straightforward: if your ERP or AI tools are making decisions about customers, your Privacy Policy needs to say so — clearly, specifically, and by December 2026.
