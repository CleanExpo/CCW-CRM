# What Must You Disclose? Privacy Act Obligations for Australian Trade Businesses

In [Part 1 of this series](/blog/privacy-act-2026-part-1), we covered the three deadlines every trade business needs to know. Now it's time for the practical bit: what exactly do you have to do? The 13 Australian Privacy Principles (APPs) sound like a lawyer's reading list -- and honestly, they are. But you don't need a law degree to get compliant. You need a checklist, some plain English, and about an hour of focused work.

The OAIC isn't mucking about, either. In January 2026, they launched their first-ever compliance sweep, reviewing 60 businesses in sectors that collect personal information face-to-face ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/privacy-compliance-sweep-to-put-privacy-policies-under-the-spotlight), 2026). Carpet cleaners visiting customers' homes? That's exactly the profile they're watching.

> **TL;DR:** Once covered by the Privacy Act, your trade business must comply with 13 Australian Privacy Principles. The big four obligations: publish a compliant privacy policy (APP 1), issue collection notices when you gather customer data (APP 5), secure that data with reasonable measures (APP 11), and -- from 10 December 2026 -- disclose any automated decision-making in your privacy policy (new APP 1.8). Australia recorded a record 1,113 data breaches in 2024, up 25% on the prior year ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/oaic-stats-show-record-year-for-data-breaches), 2025). Getting this right matters.

---

## What Is an APP Entity -- and Are You One?

From 1 July 2026, over 100,000 Australian small businesses will become "APP entities" for the first time ([Helios Salinger](https://www.heliossalinger.com.au/2026/03/12/privacy-reforms-to-impact-over-100000-small-businesses/), 2026). An APP entity is simply any organisation covered by the Privacy Act 1988. If your carpet cleaning, restoration, or equipment supply business loses its small business exemption -- whether through Tranche 2 AML/CTF reforms or the broader exemption removal -- you're in.

What does that actually mean? It means all 13 APPs apply to how you collect, store, use, and disclose personal information. "Personal information" is broad: names, phone numbers, email addresses, home addresses from job bookings, payment details, even photos of a customer's property you've taken for a quote.

Haven't been captured yet? You can opt in voluntarily by registering with the OAIC. Some trade operators are doing this now to get ahead of the curve and demonstrate professionalism to commercial clients who already require privacy compliance from their contractors.

> **Worth noting:** The OAIC's compliance sweep targeted sectors that collect information face-to-face -- rental, pharmacy, licensed venues, car dealers. Carpet cleaners and restorers visiting homes fit that same risk profile perfectly, even though they weren't in the first sweep.

## Your Privacy Policy -- What Must It Contain?

APP 1 requires every APP entity to have a clearly expressed, up-to-date privacy policy that's freely available ([OAIC APP 1 Guidelines](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-1-app-1-open-and-transparent-management-of-personal-information), 2024). Entities found with non-compliant policies in the OAIC's sweep face infringement notices of up to $66,000 per contravention ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/about-the-OAIC/our-regulatory-approach/guide-to-privacy-regulatory-action/chapter-8-infringement-notices), 2025). That's not a theoretical risk -- it's what happens when the OAIC knocks.

Your privacy policy must cover, at minimum:

- **What personal information you collect** -- names, phone numbers, addresses, photos of property, payment details, ABN, vehicle details if you visit worksites
- **Why you collect it** -- to provide quotes, complete jobs, issue invoices, follow up for maintenance reminders
- **How you store it** -- cloud-based CRM, paper job sheets, accounting software, email
- **Who you share it with** -- subcontractors, equipment suppliers, accounting firm, cloud service providers
- **How customers can access and correct their data** -- email address, phone number, process
- **How to make a complaint** -- internal process first, then escalation to the OAIC
- **Whether you disclose data overseas** -- if your cloud software stores data on servers outside Australia (many do)

Where does it go? On your website, obviously. But also consider adding a summary to your booking forms, quote templates, and customer intake documents. The OAIC expects privacy policies to be accessible at the point of collection, not buried three clicks deep on a website.

## Collection Notices -- What to Say When You Gather Customer Data

APP 5 requires you to tell people why you're collecting their personal information at or before the time of collection ([OAIC APP 5 Guidelines](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/australian-privacy-principles/australian-privacy-principles-guidelines/chapter-5-app-5-notification-of-the-collection-of-personal-information), 2024). For a carpet cleaner, that means when you take a booking over the phone, when a technician fills out a job sheet on-site, or when a customer submits a quote request through your website. Human error caused 37% of all notifiable data breaches in the first half of 2025 ([OAIC NDB Report](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/blog/latest-notifiable-data-breach-statistics-for-january-to-june-2025), 2025). Proper collection practices and clear notices reduce that risk.

![Clipboard and form representing data collection at a trade business job site](https://cdn.pixabay.com/photo/2015/01/08/18/27/startup-593341_1280.jpg)

A collection notice doesn't have to be a legal document. It can be two or three sentences on a form. Here's what it must contain:

1. **Your business name and contact details**
2. **What information you're collecting**
3. **Why you're collecting it** (the primary purpose)
4. **Who you might share it with**
5. **Whether the collection is required by law**
6. **What happens if they don't provide it** (e.g., you can't complete the booking)
7. **How they can access and correct their information**

### What This Looks Like in Practice

**On a phone booking form:** A short statement your receptionist reads: "We'll collect your name, address, and phone number to schedule and complete your cleaning job. We store this in our job management system and may share it with our technicians. Our privacy policy is at [website]."

**On a website quote form:** A one-liner above the submit button: "By submitting this form, you agree to the collection of your details as described in our [privacy policy]. We use this information to prepare your quote."

**On a paper job sheet:** A printed note at the bottom: "We collect personal information to provide our services. See our privacy policy at [website] or ask our team for a copy."

Trade businesses must notify customers at the point of data collection -- whether that's a phone booking, an on-site visit, or an online form. With 37% of data breaches in H1 2025 attributed to human error ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/blog/latest-notifiable-data-breach-statistics-for-january-to-june-2025), 2025), clear collection notices and consistent processes are one of the most practical steps you can take to reduce your breach risk.

## Data Security -- What APP 11 Requires

Malicious or criminal attacks caused 59% of all data breaches in H1 2025, while human error accounted for 37% ([OAIC NDB Report](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/blog/latest-notifiable-data-breach-statistics-for-january-to-june-2025), 2025). APP 11 requires you to take "reasonable steps" to protect personal information from misuse, interference, loss, and unauthorised access. What counts as "reasonable" scales with your business size and the sensitivity of the data you hold.

For a small trade business, "reasonable steps" includes:

### Digital Security
- **Strong passwords** on all systems -- CRM, email, accounting software, cloud storage
- **Multi-factor authentication** (MFA) on anything that stores customer data
- **Encrypted storage** -- most modern cloud systems (Xero, ServiceM8, CCW) handle this, but check
- **Regular software updates** -- outdated systems are the easiest way in for attackers
- **Access controls** -- not every staff member needs access to every customer record

### Physical Security
- **Lock up paper job sheets** if you still use them
- **Shred documents** with personal information before disposal
- **Secure work vehicles** -- if a tablet or laptop with customer data is in the van, lock the van

### Staff Training
- Teach your team what personal information is and why it matters
- Run through your collection notice script so it's consistent
- Make sure everyone knows the process if a customer asks to see or correct their data

With an average of 10,000 individuals affected per cyber incident and 532 breaches reported in H1 2025 alone ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/blog/latest-notifiable-data-breach-statistics-for-january-to-june-2025), 2025), even a small trade business with a customer database of a few thousand is a viable target. Don't assume you're too small to be hit.

Reasonable security measures under APP 11 include encrypted digital storage, multi-factor authentication, strong password policies, access controls, and physical record protection. With 59% of data breaches in H1 2025 caused by malicious attacks and an average of 10,000 individuals affected per cyber incident ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/blog/latest-notifiable-data-breach-statistics-for-january-to-june-2025), 2025), small trade businesses can't afford to treat data security as someone else's problem.

## Automated Decision-Making -- The December 2026 Deadline

From 10 December 2026, if your business uses software to make or substantially assist in making decisions about individuals, you must disclose this in your privacy policy ([Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00109/latest/text), 2024). This is new APP 1.8, and it applies to any automated process that uses personal information to produce a decision affecting someone's interests.

![Laptop displaying business analytics dashboard representing automated decision-making in trade businesses](https://cdn.pixabay.com/photo/2016/11/29/06/15/plans-1867745_1280.jpg)

Does your trade business use automated decision-making? Probably more than you think:

- **Automated quoting** -- if your CRM or quoting tool calculates prices based on customer location, property size, or job history, that's ADM
- **Customer scoring or tiering** -- if your system assigns a "priority level" to customers based on their spend or payment history, that's ADM
- **AI-powered scheduling** -- if software decides which technician goes where based on an algorithm, that's ADM
- **Credit checks on trade accounts** -- if your system auto-approves or flags applications based on data, that's ADM

### What Must You Disclose?

Under the new rules, your privacy policy needs to explain:

1. **That you use automated decision-making**
2. **What kinds of personal information** feed into those systems
3. **What kinds of decisions** are made solely by automated means
4. **What kinds of decisions** are substantially assisted by automated means
5. **How someone can request a human review** of an automated decision

You've got until 10 December 2026 to audit your systems and update your privacy policy. That's roughly eight months from now. Doesn't sound urgent? Consider this: most trade businesses don't even realise their quoting software qualifies. Start the audit now.

From 10 December 2026, businesses using software for automated quoting, customer scoring, scheduling, or credit decisions must disclose these processes in their privacy policy under new APP 1.8 of the Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024. The disclosure must cover what personal information is used, what decisions the system makes, and how individuals can request human review ([Attorney-General's Department](https://www.ag.gov.au/rights-and-protections/privacy), 2024).

## Frequently Asked Questions

### Can I use a template privacy policy?

Yes -- the OAIC provides guidance and a Privacy Foundations self-assessment tool to help you write a compliant privacy policy ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/organisations/small-business), 2024). Templates are a solid starting point, but you must customise them to your actual data practices. A generic template that doesn't reflect what your business actually collects won't pass a compliance sweep. The OAIC reviewed 60 businesses in January 2026 and focused specifically on whether policies matched real practices.

### Do I need to tell customers if my quoting software uses their data?

From 10 December 2026, if your quoting software uses personal information to make or assist decisions -- like calculating a price based on property type, location, or customer history -- you must disclose this in your privacy policy under new APP 1.8 ([Privacy and Other Legislation Amendment Act 2024](https://www.legislation.gov.au/C2024A00109/latest/text)). Even if a human reviews the quote before sending, the automated component still needs disclosure if it substantially influences the outcome.

### What if I only collect names and phone numbers?

Even minimal personal information triggers the APPs. A name and a phone number count as personal information under the Privacy Act 1988. You still need a privacy policy (APP 1), a collection notice (APP 5), and reasonable security measures (APP 11). The 1,113 data breaches notified in 2024 weren't all from organisations holding sensitive health records -- plenty involved basic contact details ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/news/media-centre/oaic-stats-show-record-year-for-data-breaches), 2025).

### Do subcontractors need their own privacy policies?

If a subcontractor collects personal information independently -- not just as your agent following your instructions -- they likely need their own privacy compliance. A subbied carpet cleaner who takes their own bookings and manages their own customer list is an APP entity in their own right from July 2026. If they only work under your direction and use your systems, they're acting as your agent and your policy covers them ([OAIC](https://www.oaic.gov.au/privacy/privacy-guidance-for-organisations-and-government-agencies/organisations/small-business), 2024).

### How do I handle customer data collected before July 2026?

Personal information you collected before becoming an APP entity is still subject to the APPs once you're covered. You should audit existing records: what do you have, where is it stored, do you still need it? Under APP 11, you must destroy or de-identify personal information you no longer need for any purpose. That box of old job sheets in the shed? Time to shred it.
